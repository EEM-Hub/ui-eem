## Affordance Theory: From Ecological Psychology to Design

Affordance is a concept originating in ecological psychology that describes what an environment offers an individual — its action possibilities. Coined by James J. Gibson in 1966 and refined through his 1979 book *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, the term has been adopted and reinterpreted across fields including HCI, interaction design, robotics, fire safety, and language education. The concept's migration from psychology to design — particularly through Donald Norman's influential reinterpretation — created productive but sometimes confusing dual meanings that persist today.

## Key Concepts

- **Gibson's original definition**: Affordances are what the environment *offers, provides, or furnishes* to an animal, for good or ill. They imply complementarity between organism and environment.
- **Relational nature**: Affordances depend on the observer's current intentions, capabilities, and needs — the same object affords different things to different actors or the same actor at different times. "Needs control the perception of affordances."
- **Norman's reinterpretation (1988)**: Narrowed affordance to mean *readily perceivable action possibilities* — what an object "suggests" about how it can be used. Depends on actor's goals, beliefs, and past experience, not just physical capability.
- **Signifiers (Norman, 2013)**: Norman later acknowledged his reinterpretation was unintended and introduced "signifiers" to distinguish: affordances determine what actions are *possible*; signifiers communicate *where* action should take place.
- **Value-rich ecological object**: Affordances cannot be described in value-neutral physics language; they inherently involve benefits and injuries relative to the organism.
- **Gaver's three categories**:
  - **Perceivable affordance**: Action possibilities that are both real and perceived by the actor (direct link between perception and action).
  - **Hidden affordance**: Real action possibilities that are not perceived (e.g., using a shoe to open a wine bottle).
  - **False affordance**: Perceived action possibilities that don't actually exist (e.g., placebo buttons).
- **Davis's mechanisms and conditions framework**: Shifts from *what* technologies afford to *how* they afford, *for whom*, and *under what circumstances*. Technologies can request, demand, encourage, discourage, refuse, or allow action, conditioned on perception, dexterity, and cultural/institutional legitimacy.
- **Nguyen's five-dimensional framework** (language education): perceptibility, valence, compositionality, normativity, intentionality — all must align for an affordance to become a learning opportunity.

## Relationships

- **Ecological psychology / James J. Gibson**: Origin discipline; affordance is central to Gibson's ecological approach to perception.
- **Eleanor J. Gibson / Perceptual learning**: Extended affordance theory into developmental learning.
- **Donald Norman / *The Design of Everyday Things***: Bridge from psychology to HCI and interaction design; responsible for the "perceived action possibilities" interpretation.
- **HCI and interaction design**: Adopted Norman's version; affordance became a core design principle for making interfaces intuitive.
- **Signifiers**: Norman's corrective concept — complementary to affordances, addressing *communication* of action rather than *possibility* of action.
- **Robotics**: Practical application — robots must learn object affordances (graspability, manipulability) from visual perception and experience.
- **Fire safety**: Applied to evacuation system design — clear pathways and signage afford quick evacuation.
- **Embodied cognition, situated cognition, enactivism**: Philosophical neighbors emphasizing organism-environment coupling.
- **Activity theory, cognitive ecology**: Related frameworks for understanding human interaction with environments and tools.
- **Form follows function**: Design philosophy that aligns with affordance-based thinking.

## Exam-Relevant Points

- **Gibson coined "affordance" in 1966**, with the canonical definition appearing in his 1979 book *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*.
- **Gibson's definition is environment-centered and objective** (what is physically possible); **Norman's is actor-centered and subjective** (what is readily perceived as possible).
- **Norman introduced affordance to HCI in 1988** via *The Design of Everyday Things* — but later clarified this was an unintended narrowing.
- **Signifiers ≠ affordances**: Affordances = what's possible; signifiers = what communicates where/how to act. Norman added "signifiers" in his 2013 revision.
- **Gaver's three categories** (perceivable, hidden, false) are a standard classification — know examples of each.
- **False affordance** = perceived but nonexistent possibility (placebo button is the canonical example).
- **Affordances are relational, not properties of objects alone** — they exist at the intersection of actor capabilities/intentions and environment features.
- **Davis's framework** introduced six mechanisms (request, demand, encourage, discourage, refuse, allow) and three conditions (perception, dexterity, cultural/institutional legitimacy).
- **Anderson et al. (2002)**: Merely looking at an object primes the brain to perform the action it affords — links perception directly to action.
- **Children learn affordances as socialization** — learning conventional object use is how they enter shared social practices.
