## Animation: Principles, History, and Production

Animation is a filmmaking technique in which images are created or manipulated and played in sequence to produce the illusion of moving pictures. This page covers the full scope of animation — from its optical-science foundations and pre-cinema mechanical devices, through the golden age of hand-drawn cel animation, to the modern dominance of computer-generated imagery (CGI). It also addresses animation's economic significance, its use beyond entertainment (propaganda, education, advertising), and the major award categories recognizing the medium.

## Key Concepts

- **Animation defined**: Sequential display of images creating the illusion of motion; contrasted with live action, though hybrids exist.
- **Persistence of vision / phi phenomenon / beta movement**: Historically cited perceptual explanations for why animation works; exact neurological causes remain uncertain. The actual effect is **stroboscopic**.
- **Traditional (cel) animation**: Images hand-drawn or painted on transparent celluloid sheets, photographed frame-by-frame onto film. Patented by John Randolph Bray and Earl Hurd; dominated the industry for most of the 20th century.
- **Computer animation**: Includes detailed 3D animation and 2D computer animation that can emulate traditional styles. Based on programming paths between **key frames**.
- **Stop motion**: Physically manipulating objects (clay, puppets, paper cutouts) frame-by-frame. Includes claymation, silhouette animation, and pinscreen animation.
- **Limited animation**: Cost-reduced technique developed for TV, using fewer frames and simpler movements — driven by the volume demands of American television programming.
- **Animated cartoon / "toon"**: An animated film (usually short) with exaggerated visual style, often featuring anthropomorphic characters. Term "toon" popularized by *Who Framed Roger Rabbit* (1988).
- **CAPS (Computer Animation Production System)**: Developed by Pixar and Disney in the late 1980s; enabled *The Rescuers Down Under* (1990) as the first feature film created entirely digitally without a camera.
- **Market size**: ~US$80 billion in 2010, growing to ~US$370 billion by 2021. Animated features had the highest gross margins (~52%) of all film genres from 2004–2013.

## Commands and Syntax

Not applicable — this is a conceptual/historical topic rather than a technical procedures document. However, key **production terminology** includes:

- **Frames per second (FPS)**: Rate at which sequential images are displayed (example in article: 10 FPS for simple 2D animation).
- **Key frames**: Define start/end positions of movement; computer interpolates the in-between frames.
- **Cel**: Transparent sheet on which characters are drawn, layered over painted backgrounds.
- **Compositing**: Combining animated VFX elements with live-action footage.

## Relationships

- **Filmmaking / cinematography**: Animation is a subset of filmmaking; shares traits like high production cost and labor intensity with live-action features.
- **Computer graphics / CGI**: Modern animation is largely computer-generated; 3D animation became dominant after *Toy Story* (1995).
- **Comics and manga**: Strong bidirectional relationship — characters move between animation and print media, especially in Japan (manga to anime pipeline).
- **Video games**: Interactive animation medium; characters and plots flow between games and films.
- **Visual effects (VFX)**: CGI animation techniques are used for VFX compositing in live-action films.
- **Theme parks / merchandising**: Animation IP drives enormous licensing revenue (Disney model); theme park earnings can exceed film earnings.
- **Television**: Shifted animation production from theatrical shorts to TV series starting in the 1950s; drove development of limited animation techniques.
- **Propaganda and education**: Animation's clarity and malleability make it effective for instruction and persuasion (WWII-era propaganda films, Cold War productions).

## Exam-Relevant Points

- **Phenakistoscope** (1832, Joseph Plateau, Belgium) introduced the principle of modern animation; preceded the zoetrope (1876), flip book (1868), and praxinoscope (1877).
- **Fantasmagorie** (1908, Emile Cohl) is the oldest known complete traditional hand-drawn animation on standard film.
- **El Apostol** (1917, Quirino Cristiani, Argentina) was the first feature-length animated film (now lost).
- **Die Abenteuer des Prinzen Achmed** (1926, Lotte Reiniger, Germany) is the oldest *extant* animated feature (silhouette animation).
- **Steamboat Willie** (1928) popularized synchronized sound in animation and launched Disney's dominance.
- **Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs** (1937) was Disney's first animated feature and remains one of the highest-grossing traditional animated films.
- **The Rescuers Down Under** (1990) — first feature created entirely digitally (using CAPS, no camera).
- **Toy Story** (1995, Pixar) — first fully computer-animated feature film in 3D style.
- **Beauty and the Beast** (1991) — first animated film nominated for Academy Award for Best Picture.
- **Academy Award for Best Animated Short Film** has existed since 1932; **Best Animated Feature** since 2002.
- **Felix the Cat** (1919) — first fully realized anthropomorphic animal character in American animation history.
- **Golden Age of American Animation**: ~1928 (Steamboat Willie) to ~1960s; dominated by cel-animated theatrical shorts.
- **Princess Iron Fan** (1941) — first animated feature film produced in Asia (China).
