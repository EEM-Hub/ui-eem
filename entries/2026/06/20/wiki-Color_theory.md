## Color Theory: Principles of Color Behavior and Mixing

Color theory is a historical body of knowledge describing the practical behavior of colors, encompassing color mixing, color contrast effects, color harmony, color schemes, and color symbolism. It is distinguished from color science in that it tends to be more subjective and artistically oriented, while color science is more objective and functional. The field dates back to antiquity (Aristotle, Bharata's Nāṭya Shāstra) and was formalized in the 18th century following Newton's work on optics, evolving through contributions from artists, scientists, and industrial chemists.

## Key Concepts

- **Traditional vs. modern color theory**: Traditional theory assumed three "pure" primary colors could mix all possible colors; modern color science rejects universal primaries and recognizes any set of primaries can only produce a limited **gamut**.
- **RYB color model**: Red, yellow, blue — the historical artist's primaries (first formally proposed by François d'Aguilon, 1613). These are subtractive secondary colors, not true subtractive primaries.
- **RGB color model**: Red, green, blue — additive primaries based on human cone cell physiology (Young-Helmholtz theory of trichromacy).
- **CMY/CMYK color model**: Cyan, magenta, yellow (+ black key) — true subtractive primaries used in printing and photography. Each absorbs exactly one retinal primary: cyan absorbs red, magenta absorbs green, yellow absorbs blue-violet.
- **Primary colors**: Cannot produce all perceivable colors; any set defines a color space with a limited gamut.
- **Secondary colors**: Produced by mixing two primaries (e.g., in RYB: orange, green, purple).
- **Complementary colors**: Colors opposite on the color wheel that cancel each other's hue to produce achromatic (white/gray/black). Examples: red-green, violet-yellow, blue-orange.
- **Three color attributes**: Hue, chroma (saturation), and value (lightness) — the Munsell system (1905) formalized these.
- **Tints**: Color + white. **Shades**: Color + black. Both can cause undesirable hue shifts.
- **Warm colors**: Red through yellow (including browns/tans) — appear to advance, arouse, stimulate.
- **Cool colors**: Blue-green through blue-violet (including most grays) — appear to recede, calm, relax. Peak contrast historically placed between red-orange and greenish-blue.
- **Split-primary palette**: Uses two versions of each primary ("warm" and "cool") to expand mixable gamut; flawed in principle but practically effective because the "biased" positions approximate cyan and magenta.
- **Color harmony**: A function of color interactions modulated by individual differences, cultural experience, context, perceptual effects, and temporal trends. Humans can perceive ~2.3 million colors, making predictive harmony formulas inherently limited.

## Commands and Syntax

Not applicable (this is a conceptual/historical domain, not a technical tool). However, key **color scheme formulas** are described:

- **Analogous**: Colors adjacent on the color wheel — produces monochromatic/single-hue experience.
- **Complementary**: Colors directly opposite on the wheel — maximum contrast and visual tension.
- **Split complementary**: One color plus the two colors adjacent to its complement (e.g., red + blue-green + yellow-green).
- **Triadic**: Three colors equidistant on the color wheel.

**Subtractive mixing rule**: Mixing pigments always produces a result darker and lower in chroma than the parent colors (moves toward neutral gray/black).

**Additive mixing rule**: Combining colored light produces brighter results; complementary lights combine to white.

**Newton's geometric rule**: Colors closer together on the hue circle produce more vibrant (higher saturation) mixtures.

## Relationships

- **Color theory ↔ Color science**: Historically intertwined but diverged in the 19th century; color theory is subjective/artistic, color science is objective/functional.
- **RYB ↔ CMY/CMYK**: RYB is the traditional artist model; CMY is the scientifically correct subtractive model. RYB "works" due to pigment impurities and sloped absorption curves.
- **RGB ↔ CMY**: Complementary systems — additive (light) vs. subtractive (pigment). Each CMY primary absorbs one RGB primary.
- **Young-Helmholtz theory → Colorimetry**: Trichromatic vision theory led to quantitative color measurement and color space models.
- **Munsell color system**: Bridges art and science by organizing colors along three perceptual dimensions (hue, value, chroma).
- **Chevreul's contrast laws → Color harmony**: Simultaneous, successive, and mixed contrast effects underpin harmony and color scheme decisions.
- **Opponent process theory**: Extends beyond Young-Helmholtz to explain complementary color perception and afterimages.

## Exam-Relevant Points

- **No universal primary colors exist** — any three primaries produce only a limited gamut, never the full range of human color perception.
- **Subtractive primaries are CMY, not RYB**. Red and blue are subtractive secondaries. RYB is historically important but scientifically inferior for high-chroma mixing.
- **Additive primaries are RGB**, based on human cone cell physiology (Young-Helmholtz trichromacy theory).
- **CMYK** adds black ("key") for economic dark-color reproduction in printing; it cannot reproduce all colors (notably weak in orange and purple).
- **Newton's key contribution**: White light is composed of a spectrum of colors; color is not intrinsic to objects but arises from reflection/absorption of wavelengths (1672 paper).
- **Goethe's *Theory of Colours* (1810)** and **Chevreul's *Law of Simultaneous Color Contrast* (1839)** are the two founding documents of color theory.
- **Chevreul's three contrast types**: Simultaneous (side by side), successive (afterimage on neutral background), mixed (afterimage on another color).
- **Munsell's three attributes**: Value (lightness), chroma (saturation), hue — introduced 1905.
- **Nāṭya Shāstra** (c. 200 BCE) identified four primaries: black, blue, yellow, red — and described mixing derived colors from them.
- **Adding black to darken** can shift hues toward green/blue; using a complementary color to darken avoids hue shift.
- **Adding white to lighten** reds/oranges can shift them toward blue; correct by adding a small amount of adjacent hue.
- **Warm/cool distinction** dates to at least the late 18th century, rooted in landscape light observation (daylight/sunset vs. overcast).
- **Split-primary system works in practice** because its "biased" positions approximate magenta and cyan — effectively using near-correct subtractive primaries.
- **Pantone Hexachrome** (six-color printing) extends beyond CMYK to reproduce a wider gamut.
- **Color harmony** is not purely formulaic — it depends on individual, cultural, contextual, perceptual, and temporal factors.
