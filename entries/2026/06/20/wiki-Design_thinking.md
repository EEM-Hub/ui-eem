## Design Thinking: Cognitive Processes, Innovation Methods, and Applications

Design thinking encompasses the cognitive, strategic, and practical procedures designers use when solving problems, as well as a broader methodology adopted by business and education for driving innovation. The term carries three distinct meanings: a cognitive style (thinking like a designer), a general theory of design practice, and a teachable set of methods for approaching complex problems. Its roots trace from 1950s creativity research through formalized design methods in the 1960s to widespread business adoption in the 2000s.

## Key Concepts

- **Design thinking is iterative and non-linear** — it cycles through context analysis, problem finding, ideation, prototyping, and evaluation without a fixed sequence.
- **Wicked problems** — ill-defined problems with no definitive formulation and no true/false solutions; term introduced by Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber. Contrasted with "tame" problems solvable through rules or technical knowledge. A further category of "super wicked" global problems emerged in the 2010s.
- **Problem framing** — designers do not accept problems as given; they explore, reinterpret, and restructure problems to find a framing that suggests a solution route (per Donald Schon's reflective practice model).
- **Solution-focused thinking** — designers use solution conjectures to develop understanding of the problem, unlike scientists who use problem-focused strategies (Bryan Lawson's finding).
- **Abductive reasoning** — designers infer possible solutions from incomplete information using analogies and non-deductive thinking, a form of Peirce's "innovative abduction."
- **Co-evolution of problem and solution** — understanding of the problem and ideas for solutions develop simultaneously; new solutions deepen problem understanding, which triggers further solutions.
- **Representations and modelling** — visual/object languages (sketches, drawings, computer models, physical prototypes) are central to exploring tentative solutions and recognizing emergent properties.
- **Empathy** — understanding users' psychological and emotional needs, not just physical ergonomics; foundational to human-centered design (emphasized by Tom and David Kelley).
- **Divergent and convergent thinking** — ideation alternates between generating many ideas (divergent) and selecting the best (convergent); diverse teams support divergent phases.
- **DesignThinking 1 vs. DesignThinking 2** — Nigel Cross's distinction: DT1 is the original design-cognition tradition; DT2 is the popularized business/innovation version. The business version's dominance may force the original to rebrand as "designerly thinking."

## Commands and Syntax

No CLI commands apply. The key procedural frameworks are:

**Plattner-Meinel-Leifer Five-Phase Model (Stanford d.school):**
1. (Re)define the problem
2. Needfinding and benchmarking
3. Ideating
4. Building
5. Testing

**Three Overlapping Spaces (IDEO/Tim Brown):**
1. **Inspiration** — observe real-world behavior, identify problems/opportunities, document in a brief with constraints, benchmarks, and objectives
2. **Ideation** — brainstorm, find patterns, synthesize insights into solutions
3. **Implementation** — prototype, test, evaluate, iterate, refine

**Prototyping principles:** Even rough mock-ups gather feedback, identify strengths/weaknesses, and prompt new ideas. Speed of iteration is prioritized.

## Relationships

- **Wicked problems** connect to participatory design and problem structuring methods — Rittel argued "second generation" design methods needed participatory, argumentative approaches
- **Abductive reasoning** links to Charles Sanders Peirce's logic and contrasts with deductive/inductive reasoning used in science
- **Empathic design** extends from user research and ergonomics into psychological/emotional understanding
- **Related thinking frameworks:** lateral thinking, systems thinking, reflective practice, creativity techniques
- **Design methods / design process** — design thinking is a subset or perspective within the broader design methods tradition
- **Education:** connects to design-based learning, K-12 curricula, International Baccalaureate, Maker Education, and Stanford's d.school (founded 2003)
- **Business strategy:** links to strategic design, new product development, innovation management, and service design

## Exam-Relevant Points

- **Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber** coined "wicked problems" (1973) — know the characteristics: no definitive formulation, no true/false solution, wide discrepancy between stakeholder perspectives
- **Bryan Lawson** demonstrated architects use solution-focused strategies vs. scientists' problem-focused strategies
- **Nigel Cross** — "Designerly Ways of Knowing" (1982) established design thinking as a distinct form of knowledge relevant beyond professional design
- **Peter G. Rowe** — *Design Thinking* (1987) was a significant early use of the term in design research literature
- **Richard Buchanan** — "Wicked Problems in Design Thinking" (1992) broadened design thinking to address intractable human concerns
- **Donald Schon** — *The Reflective Practitioner* (1983) established the epistemology of reflective practice in design
- The five-phase model (redefine, needfinding, ideate, build, test) is associated with **Hasso Plattner, Meinel, and Leifer** at Stanford
- **IDEO** (founded 1991 by David Kelley) was among the first consultancies to showcase design process publicly
- **Key criticism:** design thinking in business can de-politicize practice (Kimbell), devalue domain expertise in favor of shallow "creative confidence" (Vinsel), and reinforce designer privilege through uncritical empathy (Iskander)
- The design thinking timeline moves from creativity techniques (1950s) → design methods (1960s) → wicked problems/participatory design (1970s) → human-centered design (1980s) → Delft symposia/IDEO (1990s) → business popularization (2000s) → criticism and correction (2010s–2020s)
- **John E. Arnold** — *Creative Engineering* (1959) identified four areas: novel functionality, higher performance, lower production costs, increased salability
