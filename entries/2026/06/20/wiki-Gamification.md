## Gamification: Applying Game Design Elements to Non-Game Contexts

Gamification is the integration of game design elements — such as points, badges, leaderboards, and narratives — into non-game environments to increase user engagement, motivation, and productivity. It is a component of system design applied across domains including education, marketing, health, HR, crowdsourcing, and workplace productivity. The term first appeared online in 2008 and gained widespread usage around 2010.

## Key Concepts

- **Definition**: Integrating game mechanics and principles into non-game contexts to drive engagement and behavior change.
- **Core psychological drivers**: socializing, learning, mastery, competition, achievement, status, self-expression, altruism, and closure.
- **Game Element Hierarchy** (three tiers):
  - **Dynamics** (highest): broad system aspects like narrative and social interaction — never directly enter the game.
  - **Mechanics** (middle): processes that drive action — chance, turns, rewards.
  - **Components** (lowest): specific instantiations — points, quests, virtual goods.
- **Intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation**: Gamification works best when it taps intrinsic motivation (curiosity, enjoyment) rather than relying solely on external rewards.
- **Leaderboards have mixed motivational effects**: motivating when a player is close to the next level, demotivating when a player is at the bottom. Most effective when competitors are at similar performance levels.
- **Performance graphs** use an individual reference standard (self-comparison over time), unlike leaderboards which use a social reference standard (comparison to others). Individual reference fosters mastery orientation, which benefits learning.
- **Gamblification**: the merging of gambling and gamification, used by online casinos with incremental reward systems.

## Seven Core Game Elements

- **Points**: numerical progress indicators; types include experience, redeemable, and reputation points; serve as continuous, immediate feedback.
- **Badges**: visual representations of achievements; function as goals, status symbols, and social membership indicators; influence player behavior and route selection.
- **Leaderboards**: rank players by relative success; competitive progress indicators; effective only when competitors are at comparable levels.
- **Performance graphs**: display individual performance over time; foster mastery orientation rather than social comparison.
- **Meaningful stories**: narrative context that gives activities meaning beyond point accumulation; range from simple titles to complex storylines.
- **Avatars**: visual player representations chosen or created by the user; enable identity adoption and community membership.
- **Teammates**: real or virtual co-players enabling competition, cooperation, or conflict; teams working toward shared objectives combine competition and collaboration.

## Commands and Syntax

No CLI commands or technical syntax. The primary procedural framework is **Player-Centered Design** (5 steps):

1. **Know your player** — identify the target audience.
2. **Identify the mission** — define the goal.
3. **Understand human motivation** — map personality, desires, and triggers of the audience.
4. **Apply mechanics** — implement points, badges, leaderboards, etc.
5. **Manage, monitor, and measure** — ensure mechanics achieve the desired outcome with specific, realistic goals.

## Relationships

- **Game design / System design**: Gamification borrows from game design principles and is itself a component of system design.
- **Flow psychology**: Gamification aims to produce flow states — optimal engagement between boredom and anxiety.
- **Gamification of learning**: A distinct sub-field; platforms like Khan Academy and Quest to Learn apply gamification to formal education.
- **Crowdsourcing**: Gamification drives participation (e.g., Foldit for protein folding, ESP Game for image labeling).
- **User-centered design**: Player-centered design is an adaptation of UCD methodology.
- **Customer loyalty programs**: Points/tiers/challenges drive repeat purchasing (e.g., Starbucks + Foursquare).
- **HR and workplace training**: Gamified modules used by US Armed Forces, Unilever, SAP; 78% of workers in one study use game-based motivation at work.
- **Health and fitness**: Apps like Fitocracy, MyFitnessPal apply gamification to exercise and chronic disease self-management.

## Exam-Relevant Points

- **Three-tier hierarchy**: Dynamics > Mechanics > Components — know the distinction and examples of each tier.
- **Leaderboards are a double-edged sword**: motivating near the top, demotivating at the bottom; most effective among similar-level competitors.
- **Performance graphs vs. leaderboards**: individual reference standard (mastery orientation) vs. social reference standard (competitive orientation).
- **Badges serve multiple functions**: goals, feedback, status symbols, behavior influencers, and social group indicators.
- **Intrinsic motivation produces stronger gamification outcomes** than extrinsic motivation alone (2016 study finding).
- **Foldit**: 57,000 players matched or outperformed algorithmic solutions for protein structure optimization (published in Nature, 2010).
- **DevHub case study**: gamification increased online task completion from 10% to 80%.
- **University of Bonn**: gamification increased wiki contributions by 62%.
- **Term origin**: "gamification" first appeared online in 2008; gained widespread adoption ~2010.
- **Negative effects**: can build resentment and drive unsafe behavior (e.g., workers skipping bathroom breaks).
- **Platform pricing tiers**: quiz-specialist ($4–70/mo), plug-and-play gamification ($70–200/mo), bespoke custom builds (~$300K).
