## Prototypes: Types, Characteristics, and Applications Across Disciplines

A prototype is an early sample, model, or release of a product built to test a concept or process. The term applies across semantics, design, electronics, software engineering, metrology, and natural sciences. Prototyping bridges the gap between formalization of an idea and its evaluation, providing specifications for a real working system rather than a theoretical one.

## Key Concepts

- **Definition**: An early sample or model used to evaluate a new design, enhance precision, and provide specifications for a working system
- **Etymology**: From Greek *prototypon* ("primitive form") — *protos* ("first") + *typos* ("impression")
- **Materialization**: The step between formalizing and evaluating an idea in some design workflow models
- **Prototypicality**: How much something conforms to the expected norm of its category; analogous to stereotypes and archetypes
- **Cost tradeoff**: Individual prototype costs are substantially greater than final production costs due to inefficiencies in materials and processes
- **Risk reduction**: Prototypes reduce but cannot eliminate the risk that a design will not perform as intended
- **Fidelity gap**: A prototype may fail while the production design is sound (or vice versa) due to differences in materials, processes, and design fidelity

## Six Prototype Types

| Type | Purpose | Fidelity |
|------|---------|----------|
| **Proof-of-principle** | Verify key functional aspects | Partial functionality |
| **Working** | Represent all or nearly all functionality | High functional fidelity |
| **Visual** (incl. form study) | Represent size and appearance only | Appearance only |
| **User experience** | Support user research | Enough appearance + function for testing |
| **Functional** | Capture both function and appearance | High (may differ in scale/technique) |
| **Paper** | Early testing of software UI design | Low fidelity, hand-drawn or printed |

## Three Key Differences: Prototype vs. Final Product

- **Material**: Prototypes may use substitute materials because final materials are expensive, difficult to fabricate, or still under development
- **Process**: Mass-production processes (e.g., injection molding) are unsuitable for small quantities; prototypes use alternative fabrication (machining, stereolithography)
- **Verification**: Final products undergo statistical QA testing; prototypes receive closer individual inspection with expected adjustment/rework

## Commands and Syntax

Not applicable (general engineering/design concept). However, relevant tools and platforms mentioned:

- **Electronics prototyping**: Breadboard, stripboard, perfboard, prototype PCBs
- **Open-source tools**: Fritzing (documenting electronic prototypes), Arduino (microcontroller prototyping platform)
- **Software prototyping**: Alpha grade (first functional version) → Beta grade (feature-complete testing)
- **Rapid prototyping / RAD**: Techniques for building partial prototypes quickly to test high-risk design areas first
- **Computer modeling**: Virtual prototyping replacing physical prototypes (e.g., Boeing 787 was first physically realized on the production line)

## Relationships

- **Rapid Prototyping / Rapid Application Development**: Techniques that implement partial designs to solve problems before building the full product
- **Software Release Cycle**: Alpha → Beta progression maps directly to prototype maturation
- **Minimum Viable Product (MVP)**: Related concept — smallest version of a product that delivers value
- **Technology Demonstrator**: A proof-of-concept prototype specifically for new technology or future products
- **Testbed**: A platform and environment for rigorous experimentation, used in large development projects
- **Function Prototype (programming)**: In C/C++, a declaration of a subroutine — distinct from software prototyping
- **Prototype-based Programming**: OOP paradigm where new objects are created by cloning existing "prototype" objects
- **Prototype Pattern**: A software design pattern based on the same cloning concept
- **3D Printing**: Modern manufacturing technique closely tied to rapid prototyping
- **Human-Computer Interaction**: Uses specialized prototypes including "Wizard of Oz," role prototypes, and paper prototypes

## Exam-Relevant Points

- A **proof-of-principle** prototype verifies key functions but lacks full functionality; a **working** prototype represents all or nearly all functionality — know the distinction
- The three dimensions where prototypes differ from final products: **material, process, verification**
- **Paper prototypes** are specifically for early software UI testing and are part of software walkthroughs
- In **metrology**, a prototype was a physical artifact used as a measurement standard; the last SI prototype (the kilogram) was retired **May 20, 2019**, replaced by a definition based on the Planck constant
- **Data prototyping** produces cleansing/transformation rules and a resulting dataset — used in data migration and integration projects
- In **prototype-based programming** (e.g., JavaScript), new objects are created by cloning existing prototype objects — distinct from class-based OOP
- A **"Wizard of Oz" prototype** fakes computer interaction with a hidden human operator — useful for voice interfaces and hard-to-engineer functionality
- **Alpha software** = first functional version with limited features; **Beta software** = feature-complete version undergoing broad testing
- Prototype costs are **always higher** than production costs; prototypes are also used to **optimize and reduce** final production costs
- **Rapid prototyping** addresses the problem that building full designs repeatedly is expensive and time-consuming
