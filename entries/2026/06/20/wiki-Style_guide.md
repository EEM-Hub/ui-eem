## Style Guides: Standards for Document Writing and Design

A style guide is a set of standards governing the writing, formatting, and design of documents. Ranging from short style sheets (a few pages) to full style manuals (book-length), these guides ensure consistency within and across documents by prescribing rules for punctuation, capitalization, citations, number formatting, typography, and more. They serve general, discipline-specific, or organizational ("house style") purposes.

## Key Concepts

- **Style guide** — a set of standards for writing, formatting, and design of documents; improves communication through consistency.
- **Three types**: comprehensive (general use), discipline-specific (academic, medical, legal, journalistic, governmental), and house/corporate (created by a specific publisher or organization).
- **Cascading model** — individual project style sheets cascade over house style, which cascades over industry-wide manuals, which cascade over the language's orthographic norms (analogous to CSS cascading).
- **Scope coverage** — may address writing style, usage, language composition, visual composition, orthography, typography, ethics (authorship, research ethics), regulatory compliance, and accessibility.
- **Translation style guides** — enforce consistent grammar, tone, and localization decisions including units of measure.
- **Web style guides** — extend traditional conventions to account for digital behavior such as screen reading, emphasizing brevity, terminology, tone, structure, and user experience across multiple channels.

## Commands and Syntax

No commands or configuration syntax apply — this is a conceptual/reference topic. However, the key operational procedure is:

- **To apply a style guide**: identify your field/industry guide first, then layer your organization's house style on top, then apply any project-specific style sheet. Resolve conflicts by giving precedence to the most specific (innermost) layer.

## Relationships

- **Citation styles** — style guides define citation formats (APA, Chicago, Bluebook, OSCOLA, etc.), directly connecting to bibliography and reference management.
- **Orthography and language norms** — style guides sit atop and may adapt national spelling/grammar conventions (British vs. American English, etc.).
- **Technical and scientific nomenclature** — specialized guides govern taxonomy, chemical nomenclature, and gene nomenclature.
- **Accessibility standards** — modern style guides increasingly incorporate accessibility conventions for inclusive communication.
- **Web development** — web style guides intersect with CSS/design systems and UX writing practices.
- **Ethics and compliance** — academic/technical style guides connect to research ethics, authorship standards, and regulatory compliance frameworks.

## Exam-Relevant Points

- **Three categories**: comprehensive, discipline-specific, and house/corporate — know examples of each.
- **Major style guides by field**: AP Stylebook (journalism), Chicago Manual of Style (general academic), APA (psychology/social sciences), Bluebook/OSCOLA (law), USGPO (U.S. government).
- **Cascading hierarchy**: project style sheet → house style → industry manual → language orthography. Most specific layer takes precedence.
- **Revision cycles vary**: AP Stylebook is revised every two years; Chicago is on its 18th edition; APA and ASA are each on their 7th edition.
- **Web style guides** prioritize brevity, terminology, syntax, tone, structure, typography, graphics, and error handling — shaped by screen-reading behavior.
- **Style guides increasingly address**: inclusive language (avoiding racism, sexism, homophobia), accessibility for disabilities, and localization for translations.
