## User-Centered Design (UCD)

User-centered design (UCD), also called user-driven development (UDD), is a framework of design processes that places users, their characteristics, tasks, workflows, and environments at the center of every stage of product development. It emphasizes iterative testing — from initial requirements through post-production — to ensure products are usable, effective, and aligned with real user needs rather than forcing users to adapt to the product.

## Key Concepts

- **Core definition**: UCD is a process framework where usability goals, user characteristics, environment, tasks, and workflow receive extensive attention at every design stage.
- **Fundamental philosophy**: Optimize the product around how users actually engage with it, rather than forcing users to change their behavior to accommodate the product.
- **Priority hierarchy**: Users first, then product context/objectives/operating environment, then granular task details (development, organization, flow).
- **Cognitive science foundation**: UCD applies cognitive science principles to understand users' mental processes, behaviors, and needs, creating intuitive and efficient products.
- **Personas**: Archetypes representing target users, synthesized from ethnographic interviews, capturing behavior patterns, goals, skills, attitudes, and environment.
- **Norman's four design principles**:
  - Simplify task structure so possible actions are intuitive at any moment
  - Make things visible (conceptual model, actions, results, feedback)
  - Achieve correct mappings between intended results and required actions
  - Embrace and exploit system constraints
- **Term origin**: Coined by Rob Kling in 1977; popularized by Don Norman's 1986 book *User-Centered System Design* and 1988 book *The Design of Everyday Things*.

## Commands and Syntax

No commands per se, but a defined **four-phase UCD process**:

1. **Specify context of use** — Identify primary users, their reasons for use, requirements, and usage environment.
2. **Specify requirements** — Identify detailed technical requirements; aids in planning development and setting goals.
3. **Create design solutions and development** — Build iterative cycles of product testing and refinement based on goals and requirements.
4. **Evaluate product** — Perform usability testing and collect user feedback at every design stage.

**Research methods used in UCD**:
- Investigative: ethnographic study, contextual inquiry, prototype testing, usability testing
- Generative: card sorting, affinity diagramming, participatory design sessions
- Analytical: comparative analysis of similar usable products

## Relationships

- **Related design models**: Cooperative design (Scandinavian tradition, since 1970), participatory design (North American, since 1990), contextual design (customer-centered, real-world data gathering)
- **Distinct from**: Use-centered design and usage-centered design (related but different philosophies); human-centered design (broader umbrella)
- **Connects to**: Usability, interaction design, user experience design, information architecture, design thinking
- **Standards**: ISO 13407:1999 (human-centred design processes) and ISO 9241-210:2010 (human-centred design for interactive systems)
- **Compatible with**: Waterfall, agile, and other software engineering methodologies — UCD phases adapt to the chosen development model
- **Key figures**: Rob Kling (coined the term), Don Norman (popularized through books and UCSD research lab)

## Exam-Relevant Points

- UCD was coined by **Rob Kling in 1977**, not by Don Norman (common misconception). Norman popularized it through his 1986 and 1988 books.
- The **four-phase UCD process** (context of use, requirements, design solutions, evaluation) is iterative — phases repeat to refine the product.
- **Six principles** of user-centered design: explicit understanding of users/tasks/environments; user involvement throughout; user-centered evaluation drives design; iterative process; addresses whole user experience; multidisciplinary team.
- UCD differs from other philosophies by **optimizing the product around user behavior** rather than requiring users to adapt.
- **Personas** are synthesized from ethnographic interviews (not invented from assumptions) and include behavior patterns, goals, skills, attitudes, and environment.
- Relevant ISO standards: **ISO 13407:1999** and its successor **ISO 9241-210:2010**.
- Norman later revisited his own principles in *Emotional Design*, acknowledging his earlier work was "overly reductive" — signaling that aesthetics and emotion matter too, not just pure usability.
