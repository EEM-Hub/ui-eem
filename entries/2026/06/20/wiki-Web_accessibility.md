## Web Accessibility Standards and Legal Requirements

Web accessibility (eAccessibility) is the inclusive practice of removing barriers that prevent people with disabilities from interacting with websites. It addresses visual, motor, auditory, seizure-related, and cognitive disabilities, and through the curb cut effect, also benefits users with situational disabilities (e.g., one-handed while holding a baby) or limited bandwidth. When sites follow accessibility standards, all users gain equal access without reducing usability for non-disabled users.

## Key Concepts

- **Five disability categories addressed**: visual (blindness, low vision, color blindness), motor/mobility (tremors, paralysis), auditory (deafness, hearing impairment), seizures (photoepileptic), and cognitive/intellectual (dyslexia, PTSD, Alzheimer's)
- **Situational disability**: temporary context-based limitations (e.g., bright sunlight, carrying a child) — accessibility is not only for permanent disabilities
- **Curb cut effect**: accessibility features designed for disabled users benefit everyone
- **WCAG 2.0 four principles**: Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust (POUR)
- **WCAG 2.0 structure**: 4 principles, 12 guidelines (untestable goals), 65 testable success criteria
- **Three W3C guideline sets**: WCAG (content), ATAG (authoring tools), UAAG (user agents/browsers)
- **ISO/IEC 40500:2012**: the ISO codification of WCAG 2.0, adopted internationally (including Japan's JIS X 8341-3:2016)
- **EN 301 549**: European standard for ICT accessibility, published by ETSI, referenced by the EU Web Accessibility Directive
- **93% of web accessibility practitioners** received no formal schooling on web accessibility (2018 WebAIM survey)

## Assistive Technologies

- **Screen readers** — synthesized speech output for blind/vision-impaired users
- **Refreshable braille displays** — tactile braille output terminals
- **Screen magnification software** — enlarges display for low-vision users
- **Speech recognition software** — voice commands and dictation for motor-impaired users
- **Keyboard overlays** — physical aids for motor control difficulties
- **Captioned/sign-language video** — for deaf and hard-of-hearing users

## Key Accessible Design Practices

- Semantic HTML with text equivalents for images
- Meaningful link names (not "click here")
- Links differentiated by underline, not color alone (for color blindness)
- Large/enlargeable text and images
- Large clickable areas for imprecise mouse control
- Full keyboard navigability / switch access support
- Closed captions, chapters, and sign language for video
- Avoiding or making optional flashing/strobe effects
- Plain language with instructional diagrams

## Relationships

- **WCAG** is the central standard; most national laws reference WCAG 2.0 Level AA
- **ATAG** governs authoring tools (CMSs, editors) — ensures they produce accessible output and are themselves accessible
- **UAAG** governs browsers and media players — ensures user control over rendering
- **W3C WAI** (Web Accessibility Initiative) publishes all three guideline sets
- **EU Web Accessibility Directive** references EN 301 549, which in turn aligns with WCAG 2.0/2.1
- **European Accessibility Act (EAA)** extends beyond public sector to private sector products/services (effective June 28, 2025)
- Web accessibility connects to broader disability rights, universal design, and inclusive design movements

## Legislation by Jurisdiction

| Jurisdiction | Key Law/Standard | WCAG Level | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| **USA** | Section 508, ADA | WCAG 2.0 AA | Federal agencies; private via ADA litigation |
| **EU** | Web Accessibility Directive (2016); EAA (2019) | EN 301 549 / WCAG 2.1 | Public sector; EAA extends to private (2025) |
| **Canada** | Standard on Web Accessibility; Accessible Canada Act (2019) | WCAG 2.0 AA | Federal government websites |
| **Australia** | Disability Discrimination Act 1992 | WCAG-based | Public and private; landmark SOCOG case (2000) |
| **India** | GIGW; RPwD Act 2016 | WCAG 2.0 Level A | Government websites; broader ICT via RPwD |
| **Japan** | JIS X 8341-3:2016 | Identical to ISO/IEC 40500 | Voluntary but widely adopted |
| **Norway** | Anti-discrimination Accessibility Act (2008) | WCAG 2.0 AA | ICT aimed at general public |
| **Italy** | Stanca Act (2004, updated 2013) | WCAG 2.0 | Public sector |
| **Brazil** | e-MAG | WCAG 2.0 | Federal government websites |
| **Argentina** | Law 26.653 (2010) | Standards set by ONTI | Public sector and public-service companies |

## Exam-Relevant Points

- WCAG 2.0 was published December 11, 2008; became ISO/IEC 40500:2012 in 2012; WCAG 2.1 released in 2018
- WCAG 2.0 has **4 principles, 12 guidelines, 65 success criteria** — principles and guidelines are stable; techniques are updated periodically
- The **SOCOG case (2000, Australia)** was the first successful web accessibility lawsuit under disability discrimination law
- **Three W3C accessibility guideline sets**: WCAG (content), ATAG (authoring tools), UAAG (user agents)
- ATAG has **28 checkpoints**; addresses both accessible output and accessible tool interfaces
- The **European Accessibility Act** enters force **June 28, 2025**, extending accessibility requirements to private-sector products and services
- Canada's **Web Experience Toolkit (WET)** is open source and available for anyone to use
- Compliance with WCAG is a **legal requirement** primarily in North America, Europe, parts of South America, and parts of Asia
- WCAG was criticized for insufficient coverage of **learning disabilities and cognitive limitations** (formal objection led by Lisa Seeman, signed by 40 organizations)
- India's GIGW requires **WCAG 2.0 Level A** (notably lower than most countries' Level AA requirement)
