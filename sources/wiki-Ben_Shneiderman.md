**Ben Shneiderman** (born August 21, 1947) is an American [computer scientist](./Computer_scientist), a Distinguished University Professor in the University of Maryland Department of Computer Science, which is part of the [University of Maryland College of Computer, Mathematical, and Natural Sciences](./University_of_Maryland_College_of_Computer,_Mathematical,_and_Natural_Sciences) at the [University of Maryland, College Park](./University_of_Maryland,_College_Park), and the founding director (1983-2000) of the [University of Maryland Human-Computer Interaction Lab](./University_of_Maryland_Human-Computer_Interaction_Lab). He conducted fundamental research in the field of [human–computer interaction](./Human–computer_interaction), developing new ideas, methods, and tools such as the [direct manipulation interface](./Direct_manipulation_interface), and his eight rules of design.[[1]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-1)

 

## Early life and education

 

Born in New York, Shneiderman attended the [Bronx High School of Science](./Bronx_High_School_of_Science), and received a BS in Mathematics and [Physics](./Physics) from the [City College of New York](./City_College_of_New_York) in 1968. He then studied at the [State University of New York at Stony Brook](./State_University_of_New_York_at_Stony_Brook), where he earned an MS in Computer Science in 1972 and a PhD in 1973.

 

## Career

 

Shneiderman started his academic career at the [State University of New York at Farmingdale](./Farmingdale_State_College) in 1968 as an instructor at the Department of Data Processing. In the year before his graduation, he was an instructor in the Department of Computer Science at [Stony Brook University](./Stony_Brook_University) (then called the State University of New York at Stony Brook). In 1973, he was appointed assistant professor at the [Indiana University](./Indiana_University) Department of Computer Science. In 1976, he moved to the University of Maryland. He started as an assistant professor in the Department of Information Systems Management and became an associate professor in 1979. In 1983, he moved to the Department of Computer Science as an associate professor and was promoted to full professor in 1989. In 1983 he was the Founding Director of its [Human-Computer Interaction Lab](./University_of_Maryland_Human_–_Computer_Interaction_Lab), which he directed until 2000.[[2]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-2)

 

In 2002, his book *Leonardo's Laptop: Human Needs and the New Computing Technologies* won the [IEEE-USA Award for Distinguished Contributions Furthering Public Understanding of the Profession](./IEEE-USA_Award_for_Distinguished_Contributions_Furthering_Public_Understanding_of_the_Profession). His 2016 book, *The New ABCs of Research: Achieving Breakthrough Collaborations*, encourages combining applied and basic research. In 2019, he published *Encounters with HCI Pioneers: A Personal History and Photo Journal*; in 2022, he published *Human-Centered AI*.[[3]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-HCAIbook-3)

 

## Awards and honors

 

Shneiderman was inducted as a [Fellow](./Fellow) of the [Association for Computing Machinery](./Association_for_Computing_Machinery) in 1997, a [Fellow](./Fellow) of the [American Association for the Advancement of Science](./American_Association_for_the_Advancement_of_Science) in 2001, a Member of the [National Academy of Engineering](./National_Academy_of_Engineering) in 2010, an [IEEE Fellow](./IEEE_Fellow) in 2012,[[4]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-4) and a Fellow of the [National Academy of Inventors](./National_Academy_of_Inventors) in 2015.[[5]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-5) He is an ACM CHI Academy Member and received their Lifetime Achievement Award in 2001.[[6]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-6) He received the [IEEE Visualization Career Award](./IEEE_Visualization#Career_Award) in 2012 and was inducted into the [IEEE VIS Academy](./Technical_Committee_on_Visualization_and_Graphics#IEEE_Visualization_Academy) in 2019. In 2021, he received the InfoVis Conference Test of Time Award[[7]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-7) with coauthors [Ben Bederson](./Ben_Bederson) and [Martin M. Wattenberg](./Martin_M._Wattenberg).

 

He received Honorary Doctorates from the [University of Guelph](./University_of_Guelph) (Canada) in 1995, the [University of Castile-La Mancha](./University_of_Castile-La_Mancha) (Spain) in 2010,[[8]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-8) [Stony Brook University](./Stony_Brook_University) in 2015,[[9]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-9) the [University of Melbourne](./University_of_Melbourne) in 2017, [Swansea University](./Swansea_University) (in Wales, UK) in 2018, and the [University of Pretoria](./University_of_Pretoria) (in South Africa) in 2018.

 

## Personal life

 

Shneiderman resides in [Bethesda, Maryland](./Bethesda,_Maryland). He is the nephew of photographer [David Seymour](./David_Seymour_(photographer)).[[10]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-10)

 

## Work

 

### Nassi–Shneiderman diagram

 [![](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/40/Multiple_Branching.svg/330px-Multiple_Branching.svg.png)](./File:Multiple_Branching.svg)Example of a Nassi–Shneiderman diagram 

In the 1973 article "Flowchart techniques for structured programming" presented at a 1973 [SIGPLAN](./SIGPLAN) meeting [Isaac Nassi](./Isaac_Nassi) and Shneiderman argued:

 

With the advent of structured programming and GOTO-less programming a method is needed to model computation in simply ordered structures, each representing a complete thought possibly defined in terms of other thoughts as yet undefined. A model is needed which prevents unrestricted transfers of control and has a control structure closer to languages amenable to structured programming. We present an attempt at such a model.[[11]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-11)

 

The new model technique for [structured programming](./Structured_programming) they presented has become known as the [Nassi–Shneiderman diagram](./Nassi–Shneiderman_diagram), a graphical representation of structured software design.[[12]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-12)

 

### Flowchart research

 

In the 1970s, Shneiderman continued to study programmers and the use of [flow charts](./Flow_chart). In the 1977 article "Experimental investigations of the utility of detailed flowcharts in programming", Shneiderman et al. summarized the origin and status quo of flowcharts in [computer programming](./Computer_programming):

 

Flowcharts have been a part of computer programming since the introduction of computers in the 1940s. In 1947 Goldstein and von Neumann [7] presented a system of describing processes using operation, assertion, and alternative boxes. They felt that "coding begins with the drawing of flow diagram." Prior to coding, the algorithm had been identified and understood. The flowchart represented a high level definition of the solution to be implemented on a machine. Although they were working only with numerical algorithms, they proposed a programming methodology which has since become standard practice in the computer programming field.
[[13]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-13)

 

Furthermore, Shneiderman had conducted experiments which suggested that [flowcharts](./Flowcharts) were not helpful for writing, understanding, or modifying computer programs. At the end of their 1977 paper, Shneiderman et al. concluded:

 

Although our original intention was to ascertain under which conditions detailed flowcharts were most helpful, our repeated negative results have led us to a more skeptical opinion of the utility of detailed flowcharts under modern programming conditions. We repeatedly selected problems and tried to create test conditions which would favor the flowchart groups, but found no statistically significant differences between the flowchart and non-flowchart groups. In some cases the mean scores for the non-flowchart groups even surpassed the means for the flowchart groups. We conjecture that detailed flowcharts are merely a redundant presentation of the information contained in the programming language statements. The flowcharts may even be at a disadvantage because they are not as complete (omitting declarations, statement labels, and input/output formats) and require many more pages than do the concise programming language statements.[[14]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-14)

 

### *Designing the User Interface*

 

In 1986, he published the first edition (now in its sixth) of his book "Designing the User Interface: Strategies for Effective Human-Computer Interaction". Included in this book is his most popular list of "Eight Golden Rules of Interface Design", which reads:

  
1. Strive for consistency. Consistent sequences of actions should be required in similar situations ...
2. Enable frequent users to use shortcuts. As the frequency of use increases, so do the user's desires to reduce the number of interactions ...
3. Offer informative feedback. For every operator action, there should be some system feedback ...
4. Design dialog to yield closure. Sequences of actions should be organized into groups with a beginning, middle, and end ...
5. Offer simple error handling. As much as possible, design the system so the user cannot make a serious error ...
6. Permit easy reversal of actions. This feature relieves anxiety, since the user knows that errors can be undone ...
7. Support internal locus of control. Experienced operators strongly desire the sense that they are in charge of the system and that the system responds to their actions. Design the system to make users the initiators of actions rather than the responders.
8. Reduce short-term memory load. The limitation of human information processing in short-term memory requires that displays be kept simple, multiple page displays be consolidated, window-motion frequency be reduced, and sufficient training time be allotted for codes, mnemonics, and sequences of actions.[[15]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-15)

 

These guidelines are frequently taught in courses on Human-Computer Interaction.

 

### *The Craft of Information Visualization: Readings and Reflections,* 2003

 

In 2003, [Ben Bederson](./Ben_Bederson) and Shneiderman coauthored the book "The Craft of Information Visualization: Readings and Reflections". Included in Chapter 8: Theories for Understanding Information Visualization in this book are five goals of theories for HCI practitioners and researchers, which are:

  

The typical goals of theories are to enable practitioners and researchers to: 

 
1. Describe objects and actions in a consistent and clear manner to enable cooperation
2. Explain processes to support education and training
3. Predict performance in normal and novel situations so as to increase the chances of success
4. Prescribe guidelines, recommend best practices, and caution about dangers
5. Generate novel ideas to improve research and practice.[[16]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-16)

 

These goals are frequently taught in courses on Human-Computer Interaction and cited in works by authors such as [Yvonne Rogers](./Yvonne_Rogers), Victor Kaptelinin, and [Bonnie Nardi](./Bonnie_Nardi).

 

### Direct manipulation interface

 

Shneiderman's cognitive analysis of user needs led to principles of [direct manipulation interface](./Direct_manipulation_interface) design in 1982: (1) continuous representation of the objects and actions, (2) rapid, incremental, and reversible actions, and (3) physical actions and gestures to replace typed commands, which enabled designers to craft more effective graphical user interfaces. He applied those principles to design innovative user interfaces such as the highlighted selectable phrases in text, that were used in the commercially successful Hyperties.[[17]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-17) Hyperties was used to author the world's first electronic scientific journal issue, which was the July 1988 issue of the *[Communications of the ACM](./Communications_of_the_ACM)*[[18]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-18) with seven papers from the 1987 Hypertext conference. It was made available on a floppy disk that accompanied the printed journal. [Tim Berners-Lee](./Tim_Berners-Lee) cited this disk as the source for his "hot spots" in his Spring 1989 manifesto[[19]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-19) for the [World Wide Web](./World_Wide_Web). Hyperties was also used to create the world's first commercial electronic book, *Hypertext Hands-On!* in 1988.

 

Direct manipulation concepts led to [touchscreen](./Touchscreen) interfaces for home controls, finger-painting, and the now ubiquitous small touchscreen keyboards. The development of the "Lift-off strategy"[[20]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-Potter1988-20) by [University of Maryland Human–Computer Interaction Lab](./University_of_Maryland_Human–Computer_Interaction_Lab) (HCIL) researchers enabled users to touch the screen, getting feedback as to what will be selected, adjust their finger position, and complete the selection by lifting the finger off the screen.

 

The HCIL team applied direct manipulation principles for touchscreen [home automation](./Home_automation) systems, finger-painting programs,[[21]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-21) and the double-box range sliders[[22]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-22)
that gained prominence by their inclusion in [Spotfire](./Spotfire). The visual presentation inherent in direct manipulation emphasized the opportunity for information visualization.

 

In 1997, [Pattie Maes](./Pattie_Maes) and Shneiderman had a public debate on Direct Manipulation vs. Interface Agents at CHI'97[[23]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-23) and IUI 1997 (with the IUI Proceedings showing two separate papers[[24]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-24)[[25]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-25) but no remaining internet trace of the panel.) Those events helped define the two current dominant themes in human-computer interaction:[[26]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-26) direct human control of computer operations via visual user interfaces vs delegation of control to interface agents that know the users desires and act on their behalf, thereby requiring less human attention. Their debate continues to be highly cited (with 479 citations in January 2022 for the original CHI debate[[27]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-27)), especially in user interface design communities where return debates took place at the [ACM CHI](./Conference_on_Human_Factors_in_Computing_Systems) 2017[[28]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-28) and [ACM CHI](./Conference_on_Human_Factors_in_Computing_Systems) 2021[[29]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-29) conferences.

 

### Information visualization

 

His major work in recent years has been on [information visualization](./Information_visualization), originating the [treemap](./Treemap) concept for hierarchical data.[[30]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-30) Treemaps are implemented in most information visualization tools including [Spotfire](./Spotfire), [Tableau Software](./Tableau_Software), [QlikView](./QlikView), [SAS](./SAS_(software)), [JMP](./JMP_(statistical_software)), and [Microsoft Excel](./Microsoft_Excel). Treemaps are used in hard drive exploration tools, stock market data analysis, census systems, election data, gene expression data, and data journalism. The artistic side of treemaps is on view in the Treemap Art Project.

 

He also developed dynamic query sliders with multiple coordinated displays, a key component of [Spotfire](./Spotfire), which was acquired by [TIBCO](./TIBCO) in 2007. His work continued on visual analysis tools for time series data, [TimeSearcher](./TimeSearcher?action=edit&redlink=1), high dimensional data, [Hierarchical Clustering Explorer](./Hierarchical_Clustering_Explorer?action=edit&redlink=1), and social network data, SocialAction.[[31]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-31) Shneiderman contributed to the widely used social network analysis and visualization tool [NodeXL](./NodeXL).

 

Current work deals with visualization of temporal event sequences, such as found in Electronic Health Records, in systems such as [LifeLines2](./LifeLines2?action=edit&redlink=1)[[32]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-32) and [EventFlow](./EventFlow?action=edit&redlink=1).[[33]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-33) These tools visualize the categorical data that make up a single patient history. They present an aggregated view that enables analysts to find patterns in large patient history databases.

 

### Taxonomy of interactive dynamics for visual analysis, 2012

 

In 2012, [Jeffrey Heer](./Jeffrey_Heer) and Shneiderman coauthored the article "Interactive Dynamics for Visual Analysis" in [Association for Computing Machinery](./Association_for_Computing_Machinery) Queue vol. 10, no. 2. Included in this article is a taxonomy of interactive dynamics to assist researchers, designers, analysts, educators, and students in evaluating and creating visual analysis tools. The taxonomy consists of 12 task types grouped into three high-level categories, as shown below.

 
| Data & View Specification | Visualizedata by choosing visual encodings.Filterout data to focus on relevant items.Sortitems to expose patterns.Derivevalues or models from source data. |
| --- | --- |
| View Manipulation | Selectitems to highlight, filter, or manipulate them.Navigateto examine high-level patterns and low-level detail.Coordinateviews for linked, multi-dimensional exploration.Organizemultiple windows and workspaces. |
| Process & Provenance | Recordanalysis histories for revisitation, review, and sharing.Annotatepatterns to document findings.Shareviews and annotations to enable collaboration.Guideusers through analysis tasks or stories. |

[[34]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-34)

 

### Universal usability

 

He also defined the research area of [universal usability](./Universal_usability) to encourage greater attention to diverse users, languages, cultures, screen sizes, network speeds, and technology platforms.

 

### Human-Centered AI

 

Later in his career, Shneiderman focused on Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence.[[3]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-HCAIbook-3)[[35]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-2020HCAIpaper-35) Shneiderman proposed an alternative vision of AI that emphasized the need for reliable, safe, and trustworthy systems that enable people to benefit from AI's power while remaining in control. Shneiderman emphasized the need for technologies that "augment, amplify, empower, and enhance humans rather than replace them".[[36]](./Ben_Shneiderman#cite_note-HCAIsite-36)
